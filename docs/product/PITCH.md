# The pitch

**Crew Health Check**
*Measures what it can. Shows what it can't. Teaches the crew the rest.*

NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 · Challenge: Create Health Monitoring Software for Astronauts on Space Missions · Event: 14 and 15 November 2026

This page is the front door for judges and for the team. Everything else in the package supports it. If a document disagrees with this page about the story, the demo or what we show, this page wins and the other document gets fixed.

## In thirty seconds

> Every health app shows what it can measure. Crew Health Check also shows what your ship cannot test, and what the crew does about it with nobody to call. It reads NASA's Clinical Findings Forms for the 119 conditions NASA models for exploration missions, checks each one against everything aboard (the instruments, the supplies, the procedures packed before launch and the crew's trained hands) and draws the answer on the crew member's body: a dot where this kit can measure, an open ring where the test is not aboard. Every condition also holds a plan aboard. Where the confirming test is missing, the plan starts with the best check the ship can do; where a step needs hands, the app teaches a crewmate on the body while an array of cameras checks the crewmate's work. Its medical AI runs offline on the same laptop, asks only for what the kit can do and explains the plan to the person doing it, and every plan runs with the AI switched off. Earth gets the log at the next contact, and nothing waits for it. Around it sits the crew's whole day: a short daily check with a streak, patches and a shared crew goal, practice patches for drills, a private journal, the routine at NASA's frequencies, the habitat's air against NASA's limits on a station, in transit, on the Moon or on Mars, and a **Someone needs help** button whose first steps never wait for the AI.

**The sentence a judge repeats:** "It shows what the ship can't test, then teaches a crewmate a way around it, and the cameras check his hands. No doctor, no Earth."

**The loop we demonstrate:** know the kit → ask → measure → check it another way → act aboard (the log goes to Earth last).

**The question behind every screen:** what would change the next decision, and what can this crew do about it here?

**Why it matters.** NASA's Integrated Medical Model helps planners "optimize the medical resources packed" for a mission, and under "Clinical Optimism" it "assumes that all medical conditions are correctly diagnosed by the crew" ([NASA IMM overview](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-integrated-medical-model-imm-overview/)). Crew Health Check asks the crew-side question, condition by condition: with this kit, can they? Then it answers the next one: what do they do here, when Earth is out of reach? The answers tell a crew what to do aboard, and tell a mission what to pack and whom to train. A remote clinic far from a doctor faces the same two questions.

### Why the ground cannot be a step

| Fact | In NASA's words |
|---|---|
| At the lunar South Pole, Earth is often out of sight | Earth is "visible from the lunar South Pole for only about half of every sidereal month" ([Lunar Communications and Navigation Architecture](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/lunar-communications-and-navigation-architecture.pdf), p. 2). Without relays, direct-to-Earth coverage of South Pole landing sites is "43% best case and 17% worse case" ([LCRNS, SpaceOps 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250003321/downloads/LCRNS_Esper_257.pdf), pp. 1 and 7) |
| Mars is far even when the link is up | Crews would wait "between four and 24 minutes for their messages to reach mission control, and another four to 24 minutes to receive a response" ([Space Communications: 7 Things You Need to Know](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/tech-demonstration/space-communications-7-things-you-need-to-know)). Solar conjunction comes "approximately every 26 months" ([NASA Langley, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220013418/downloads/ASCEND-Communication%20Delays%2C%20Disruptions%2C%20and%20Blackouts%20for%20Crewed%20Mars%20Missions.pdf)); in the last one NASA "will not have contact with any Mars missions" from 29 December 2025 to 16 January 2026 ([MAVEN blog](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/maven/2025/12/23/nasa-works-maven-spacecraft-issue-ahead-of-solar-conjunction/)) |
| Going home from the Moon takes days | "Rather than a three-day lunar trip, astronauts bound for Mars would be leaving our planet for roughly three years" ([NASA HRP, Distance from Earth](https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazard-distance-from-earth/)) |
| NASA's standard expects care aboard | In-mission care includes "Means of providing autonomous medical care and advanced life support" [V1 3004]; "a return to Earth may not be possible, or may take such a long time as to be rendered futile" [V1 3007, rationale] ([NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1 Rev C](https://standards.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/standards/NASA/C/nasa-std-3001-vol-1-rev-c-signature.pdf)) |
| NASA's exploration medicine team says the same | "No real-time communications + blackouts", "No evacuations possible" ([NASA ExMC, AsMA 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230007863/downloads/EIMO.AsMA2023.Lemery.final.pdf), slide 5); crews "will need to autonomously detect, diagnose, treat, and prevent medical conditions" ([NASA Ames, 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/ames/ames-science/ames-space-biosciences/a-clinical-decision-support-system-for-earth-independent-medical-operations/)) |
| Today's medical officer training leans on the ground | "approximately 15 hours total is spent on CMO training", "knowing that ground specialists will provide real-time guidance" ([NASA abstract, AsMA 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20205008925)) |

**The rule this sets:** the ground is a reader, not a step. Every plan, help flow, warning and routine task runs to its end with the link down. The log leaves at the next contact; a reply is advice for later and never unlocks anything.

## What a judge sees

**What is new**

1. **What this ship can test, and a plan aboard for each condition.** Before any reading, the body shows the kit's reach: a lime dot where this kit measures, a hollow ring where only the person's report is possible, an open dashed ring where the test that would confirm a condition is not aboard. The counts come from NASA's Clinical Findings Forms for all 119 conditions in NASA's exploration condition list, checked against the kit on the table. The same map has a plan column: for every condition, what this crew does about it here, in stages (check it another way, do now, watch, if it gets worse, the medical officer's step, on the Moon only prepare to return, and last the log for the ground). Each plan ends in one of four modes, shown as a word: **Resolve here**, **Talk a crewmate through it**, **Stabilize and watch** or, on the Moon only, **Prepare to return**. Each form also says what the ground was assumed to do for that condition and how often NASA's model expects it to end the mission (its Table 4); the plan replaces each ground task with an onboard step and shows the modeled figure as context, never as a rule. The kit view counts honestly: "[P] of 119 have a plan aboard", "[k] written for the condition, [119 minus k] from its family". Its planning view adds supplies and training beside instruments: what to pack, and whom to train. The kit map and its plans are written at the event; until every row links to its form, the project page does not claim them.
2. **Check it another way.** A dashed ring is no longer the end of the story; it is the first step of the plan. "Rhythm: needs an ECG. Not aboard." Then: "Check it another way: a crewmate feels your pulse for 60 seconds while the cameras count it. It cannot replace an ECG." The ring stays open. The check changes the watch plan, never the diagnosis.
3. **Teach and check: the cameras watch the helper's hands.** When a step needs hands, the body figure lights where they go, the step text appears one step at a time, and the camera array checks the work: a counted pulse against the camera pulse ("Your count 92 · cameras 96 · they agree") and, labeled experimental, the compression rate during CPR. No entry we found points a camera at the helper.
4. **Who helps.** A skills roster with practice recency proposes roles in an emergency: the helper, who brings the kit, the medical officer, and the fallback when the medical officer is the patient or outside on a walk. Every role states its reason and can be changed. Skills only, never health values, and no ranking.
5. **Drills earn practice patches, never scores.** The required gamification gets a second, serious job: drills of the same teach steps keep skills fresh, update the roster and earn practice patches. The camera's numbers during a drill are shown live to the trainee and never stored.

**The day around it**

6. **It asks only for what the kit can do, and keeps the failures.** "Your pulse I can measure. Your rhythm I can't: that needs an ECG." The camera array takes the pulse, and two cameras must agree. "No reading: too much movement" stays on screen, changes what happens next and is never cited as support.
7. **Every value says where it came from.** Live, Replay or Sim, the device or person that produced it, and how old it is.
8. **Today says what needs you.** Warnings rise on their own and say who raised them: a rule, a sensor limit or the assistant. The assistant can add a warning but can never lower or clear one that a rule or a sensor raised.
9. **It knows where it is.** Switch from the space station to a Moon base and the hazards, sensors, routine, kit and ground link all change: lunar dust after a surface walk, a check of eyes, skin and breathing for everyone near it, 6 to 10 seconds to Earth when the link is up and the next contact when it is down. When Earth goes out of reach, the link line is the only thing that changes. Every limit and effect cites the NASA standard or brief it came from.
10. **A routine at NASA's frequencies.** Each task says what to do step by step, how long it takes and why; frequencies come from NASA sources where they exist and read "to be set by the flight surgeon" where they do not. Nothing assumes medical training.
11. **Someone needs help works with the model switched off.** Fixed first-aid steps open at once, before any AI call; who does what appears below the steps, never before them; the assistant helps alongside; the log for the ground drafts itself and waits for the next contact.
12. **Supplies have cover, and the Moon has a way home.** Each step declares what it uses, and the app warns before a plan runs out ("cover for [n] more uses"). On the Moon only, a plan can end in preparing to return: the app lists the criteria from flight rules, NASA's modeled figures and the trip, and says who decides (the commander and the medical officer). On Mars and in transit it says "Return is not an option from here. The plan stays aboard." This stays out of the demo until flight-rule criteria exist.
13. **It rewards showing up, never the numbers.** A daily streak, mission patches and a shared crew goal count completed checks. A bad reading never costs anything, so nobody has a reason to skip a check or shade an answer. The journal is private and never read by the assistant.

**The safety frame** (what earns trust; never the headline)

- **Decisions packed before launch.** Every step, medicine mention and threshold comes from a versioned release that shows its review state. The AI never invents a step, a medicine or a dose: a proposal naming a step outside the plan, or any dose, is rejected and logged. Medicine steps read "as in the onboard procedure".
- **Every plan runs with the model switched off.** So do the teach steps, the camera checks and Someone needs help. The AI explains the plan to the person doing it; nothing waits for it.
- **Offline, with the link as the setting.** Many entries run offline and show a link badge; ours does too, and we never claim it. What we claim is what the crew does next.
- **A plan aboard, never "treats".** Many plans end in Stabilize and watch or Prepare to return, and the mode is on the card. Every clinical line carries "Unreviewed draft: needs clinician review."

## Against NASA's own work, honestly

A NASA judge may know all of this, so the project page names it first.

- **NASA's Integrated Medical Model** assumes "that all medical conditions are correctly diagnosed by the crew". The kit map answers that from the crew's side; the plan column answers the next question: and then what, here?
- **The Crew Medical Officer Digital Assistant (CMO-DA)**, from NASA JSC with Google, is "a multi-modal, agentic artificial intelligence system to support Crew Medical Officer decision-making in autonomous environments enabling Earth Independent Medical Operations", reported at "77-88% accuracy vs. physician" on structured clinical exams ([NASA, AsMA 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250004306/downloads/DocInABox_AsMA%202025_Final.pdf)). Google calls it a "proof-of-concept" ([Google Cloud, 7 August 2025](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/public-sector/how-google-and-nasa-are-testing-ai-for-medical-care-in-space)); Red Hat says it moved "to a fully disconnected, edge deployment" ([Red Hat, 24 June 2026](https://www.redhat.com/en/blog/moon-and-beyond-ramalama-being-tested-nasa-potentially-support-medical-ai-assistant-future-deep-space-missions)). A 2026 NASA abstract adds AI-guided ultrasound that cuts "the need for direct Earth-based intervention" ([NASA, AsMA 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20260001797)). **Where we stand:** CMO-DA is the direction NASA is taking, and we cite it. We do not compete on diagnostic accuracy. We build the part around any such assistant: what this kit can test and do (the kit map), the decisions packed before launch (the plans), and the crewmate's hands when the medical officer is the patient or outside (teach and check). Our assistant works inside that gate, and every plan runs with it switched off.
- **Just-in-time training already works with a ground expert watching.** On the ISS, non-physician crew "performed with high accuracy following limited preflight training and multimedia based in-flight review" while "following commands from the ground-based expert" ([Foale and others, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15945407/)). NASA's ADUM summary: "minimal crew training, just-in-time training and remote expert guidance, can be successfully used to complete complex diagnostic ultrasound exams" ([NASA ADUM briefing](https://www.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/files/issrdc_2012-06-26-1000_melton2012.pdf), slide 13). **Our step:** at the lunar South Pole, with no expert watching, the camera array watches instead, for the checks simple enough to verify by camera.
- **NASA once planned a physician for long autonomous missions.** Revision A of NASA-STD-3001 Volume 1 had a level of care for missions where "Return to Earth is not a viable option", with care "at the physician level" ([Rev A, historical](https://standards.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/standards/NASA/B/Historical/NASA-STD-3001-VOL-1A-Chg1-1.pdf)). The standard has since replaced the Levels of Care with technical requirements: Revision C asks for autonomous care [V1 3004], warns that a return to Earth may not be possible [V1 3007], and asks for at least two crew medical officers [V1 3006]. Our product covers the case when the medical officer is the patient or away.
- **EIHSO** (Earth Independent Human-Systems Operations) is about crews operating the vehicle without ground support, not about medicine, so we cite NASA's Exploration Medical Capability for the medical case.

## The project page, field by field

Drafts to finish on Sunday. The page carries most of the judges' evaluation, so it gets the same care as the video. Numbers in brackets are filled from the kit map, the plans and the result panel; none exist yet. Four images go on the page (JPG or PNG under 10 MB): the kit map on the body, a crewmate's count checked by the cameras, the plan aboard, and the result panel.

**Project name:** Crew Health Check

**High-level summary:** Crew Health Check is an offline health dashboard for astronauts that knows what its ship can and cannot test, and holds a plan aboard for the rest. From NASA's Clinical Findings Forms for the 119 conditions NASA models for exploration missions, it maps each condition to the test that would confirm it, checks that test against the kit on board and draws the answer on the crew member's body. For every condition it holds a plan the crew can run with what is aboard, starting with the best check the ship can do; when a step needs hands, it teaches a crewmate on the body while an array of cameras checks the crewmate's work. A medical AI on the same laptop asks only for what the kit can do, keeps failed readings and explains the plan, and every plan runs with it switched off. Earth gets the log at the next contact. Around it: a daily check with a streak and crew goal, practice patches for drills, a private journal, a routine at NASA's frequencies, habitat sensors against NASA limits in four mission settings, and a Someone needs help button that works with the AI off.

**Link to project demo:** the 30-second video, public, no login, checked in a private window.

**Link to final project:** the public repository, with the README walkthrough, the kit map and plan data with a link to each Clinical Findings Form, the result panel and setup instructions.

**Detailed project description** (the opening line of each section):

- **What it does.** "Crew Health Check answers four questions for each crew member: what needs me today, what can this ship test, what do we do about it here, and who helps. Its center is the kit map: for each of NASA's 119 modeled exploration conditions, the test that would confirm it, the body region it concerns, whether that test is on board, and the plan aboard."
- **The kit map and its plans.** "NASA's Integrated Medical Model helps planners choose which medical resources to fly, and it assumes every condition is correctly diagnosed by the crew. We read each condition's Clinical Findings Form and asked the crew-side question: with this kit, can they? [A] conditions get a measurement here, [B] only what the person reports, and [C] need a test this ship lacks. The same forms say what the ground was assumed to do for each condition; the plan column replaces each ground task with an onboard step. [P] of 119 have a plan aboard: [k] written for the condition, [119 minus k] from its family. Adding a single-lead ECG would fill [n] rings; an ultrasound, [m]."
- **Teach and check.** "Where the confirming test is missing, the plan starts with the best check the ship can do. A crewmate without medical training is taught on the body figure, one step at a time, and the camera array checks the work: a counted pulse against the camera pulse and, experimental, the compression rate during CPR. A skills roster with practice recency proposes who helps, including when the medical officer is the patient or outside, and drills keep those skills fresh."
- **How it works.** "A local hub on one laptop serves every screen from versioned configuration documents: the kit map, the plans, the skills and the role rules among them. The camera array, kit adapters and habitat sensors report through typed adapters as Live, Replay or Sim. Deterministic engines raise warnings, schedule the routine, compute each plan and run Someone needs help; none of them calls the model, and none of them waits for the ground. HuatuoGPT-3-9B runs offline with typed tools, and every request or plan step it proposes is checked against the kit map and the active plan before it reaches a person."
- **Beside NASA's work.** "NASA's Crew Medical Officer Digital Assistant is the direction NASA is taking for medical AI far from Earth, and we cite it. We do not compete on diagnostic accuracy: we build the part around any such assistant, which is what this kit can test and do, the decisions packed before launch, and a crewmate's hands checked by the cameras."
- **What we measured.** "At the venue, [N] adult volunteers sat at the camera station with the kit oximeter on a finger. Over [M] windows, camera pulse differed from the oximeter by [x] bpm on average; the quality check refused [y]% of windows. On [N] pairs, a crewmate's counted pulse agreed with the cameras within [tolerance] in [z]% of checks. On a manikin, the camera's compression rate differed from a metronome by [w] a minute (experimental). The AI took [t] seconds per turn on an M3 with 16 GB. On [K] frozen scenarios, rules alone did [r] and rules plus the AI did [s]. Raw results and the commands that produced them are in the repository."
- **What it cannot do.** "It diagnoses nothing and treats nothing; it holds a plan aboard. A pulse check is not an ECG, and the rhythm's ring stays open. Its clinical text, the plans included, is an unreviewed draft. Return criteria are not public and read 'to be set by flight rules'. The kit map is our reading of NASA's forms, not a clinician's, and the forms assume a physician aboard. The CO2 and radiation channels replay NASA data from 2014 and 2022 and are never anyone's exposure. The CPR rate from the cameras is experimental. The video trims waiting time."
- **Tools, languages and hardware.** Python, FastAPI, SQLite, the browser app, HuatuoGPT-3-9B, OpenCV camera workers, the kit devices, the M3 laptop. Experimental parts (the Chronos-2 forecast, face matching, band sleep scoring, the camera's CPR rate) are named here as experimental and nowhere else.

AI assistants helped with research, planning documents, interface design and code, and the product itself runs an open medical language model locally. The kit map's condition entries and plans were drafted from NASA's forms with AI help and checked by [name] against each form."
